# Exporting

From the **Render Settings** section and the buttons below it:

- **Quick export:** the export icon in the bar above the picture, next to Camera and Lenslets, opens a popup. Choose *Photo* or *Video* at the top; the popup then lists only that kind's settings, one per row: its Photo or Video settings, Quality, Upscaling and 3D. *Save* (or *Export video*) is at the bottom right, *Close* at the bottom left. They are the same settings as under Render Settings: a change in either place shows in the other.

- **Save PNG / Save JPEG** (Photo): the picture as shown, as a PNG or a JPEG (Photo *File format*, with *JPEG quality*), with all edits, crop, rotation and LUT, enlarged by the Photo *Size* (Native saves it as rendered).
- **PNG 16-bit** (Photo *File format*): the same picture with 16 bits per channel instead of 8, so the fine tonal steps survive for editing it further in another program. It is made again by the graphics card, then enlarged by the Photo *Size* with the Upscaling settings, also at 16 bits (very large sizes use 16-bit floats in between instead of 32-bit to keep the memory down); without the graphics card it falls back to an 8-bit PNG and says so. The photos themselves record 10 bits (ILLUM) or 12 bits (first generation Lytro) per pixel; the renders combine many pixels, so they hold finer steps than the sensor's.
- **JPEG HDR** (Photo *File format*): a JPEG that also keeps the highlights brighter than white. The file holds the ordinary picture, which every program shows, and a small second picture (a gain map) that says how much brighter each color is in the HDR version; programs and screens that understand it (recent Android and Chrome, Apple Photos, Adobe's programs) show the bright highlights, and the others show the ordinary picture. The highlights are the ones the picture lost: where the tone curve squeezes them near white (the Lytro look does this a little) or where they were clipped at white, up to 3 stops (8 times) brighter than the ordinary picture. How much that is depends on the photo: a picture with nothing near white looks the same as an ordinary JPEG. It is made by the graphics card, like the 16-bit PNG, and is always sRGB (*Color space* is off for it); without the graphics card an ordinary JPEG is saved and a notice says so.
- **TIFF HDR** (Photo *File format*): the HDR picture itself as a 32-bit floating-point TIFF in linear light (sRGB colors, white is 1, the highlights the picture lost are above 1), with its profile in the file, for editing in programs that read floating-point TIFFs (Photoshop, Affinity, Krita, Blender, DaVinci Resolve and others). Unlike the JPEG there is no ordinary picture in it: a program that does not handle floating-point light shows it too dark until it is told the picture is linear. The file is large: 12 bytes a pixel before compression, and a photo with grain compresses to about three quarters of that (a smooth picture much less). It is made from the same highlight map as the JPEG HDR, so the same limits apply: graphics card needed, always sRGB.
- **Color space** (Photo *Color space*): sRGB, Display P3, Adobe RGB (1998), Rec. 2020 or ProPhoto RGB. The photo is converted to it and tagged with its profile, in PNG, 16-bit PNG and JPEG files.
- **Full sensor:** turn it on (or press F), choose a *Scale* and *Detail recovery*, then *Render*. The still uses every pixel the sensor recorded, several times the resolution of the live picture. *Save PNG* (or *Save JPEG*) then saves the full sensor still.
- **Show 3D:** shows the picture in 3D while you edit, in the chosen *3D type*; turn it off for the normal view.
- **Save 3D image:** a stereo pair from two viewpoints across the lens, enlarged by the Photo *Size*: *Anaglyph* (for red / cyan glasses), *Side by side*, *Cross-eyed* or *VR headset* (side by side at full size, with `_3D_SBS` in the file name so headset players such as those on the Meta Quest recognize it; set the player to 3D side by side if it does not). *Baseline* sets how far apart the two eyes are; more gives a stronger 3D effect.
- **View in VR:** in a VR headset's web browser (such as the Meta Quest Browser, using the browser version), *View in VR* under 3D shows the 3D picture on a large screen in front of you, each eye seeing a separate view. Press a controller trigger to leave.
- **Lenticular print** (Render Settings > Lenticular Print): a picture to print and put under a lenticular lens sheet, so the print changes as it is tilted. Several pictures are cut into strips one printer dot wide and interleaved, one strip of each under every lens.
  - *Print type:* *3D* uses views across the lens (tilt the print left and right to look around the subject; for a sheet with upright lenses). *Animation* uses the Animation section's frames, evenly spaced from the first to the last: with a focus sweep, the print refocuses as it is tilted up and down (for a sheet with its lenses across). Turn on Animation and set it up first.
  - *Lens sheet:* the sheet's lenses per inch (LPI). The list has the standard sheets: 20 LPI (large format, seen from about 6 feet or more), 40 (large posters and displays, seen from beyond arm's length), 50 (short digital runs, portraits, medium-sized 3D and animation), 62 (posters, signage and displays), 75 (offset printing, promotional graphics and packaging) and 100 (small prints with fine detail and small text, seen up close). *Custom* shows a field to type any other count.
  - *Printer (DPI)*: the printer's resolution. Pictures under each lens: the printer's resolution divided by the sheet's LPI (720 / 60 = 12). The line under the settings shows the count and the file's size.
  - *Measured pitch:* sheets and printers never match their stated numbers exactly (a 60 LPI sheet may measure 59.7 to 60.3), so print *Save pitch test* first, on the same printer and paper, and look at it through the sheet: the band that flips evenly between black and white as you tilt it, without wide stripes, is the measured pitch. Enter its value; leave the field empty to use the sheet's LPI. The test is printed again for another sheet, printer or paper.
  - *Width* and *Height:* the print's size in inches; the picture fills it, centered. *Depth* (3D) is how far apart the outermost viewpoints are: more gives more depth, and more doubling out of focus.
  - *Reverse order:* for a 3D print that looks inside out (near things behind far ones), or a flip that runs the wrong way.
  - *Preview* shows the print as it looks through the sheet: drag *Tilt* (or press *Play*) to see the pictures in turn, as tilting the print shows them. *Lens detail* keeps one stripe per lens across the lenses, the detail the print has that way (a coarse sheet on a small print shows it plainly); turn it off to see the pictures at full detail. On the real print each eye sees a different picture, so the depth of a 3D print shows only there.
  - The file is a PNG at the printer's resolution, with that resolution written in it. Print it at 100% (actual size), with no scaling or "fit to page", and line the strips up with the lenses. The sheet, printer and size settings are kept on this computer for the next print; the print type and depth are saved with the photo.
- **Export video:** the animation (Animation panel, *Enabled*) as a video (in 3D when *Show 3D* is on: the chosen *3D type* and *Baseline*, both eyes for every frame) in the chosen *File format* (WebM or MP4), *Compression* (VP9, VP8, AV1, H.264 or H.265) and *Quality*, at the Video *Size* (1080p, 1440p or 4K, keeping the crop's shape, or a social media size such as 9:16 for Reels, TikTok and Shorts) and at the animation's *Frame rate*. The picture follows *Resolution*; with *Full sensor* on, every frame is rendered from the whole sensor with its *Scale* and *Detail recovery* first (much slower: each frame takes as long as a full sensor still).
- **Upscaling:** photos, 3D images and video frames are enlarged with the *Upscaling* settings under Render Settings: the *Method* (Lanczos, sharp; Smooth; FSR 1; or a downloaded upscaling model, for saved photos) and *Sharpen*. Enlarging adds pixels, not detail. See [[Render]].
- **Export album:** in the library, **•••** next to an album → *Export album…* saves the album's original photos and edits as one `.zip`, to open in another library. See [[Library and Albums]].

In the desktop app files are saved where you choose; in a browser they go to your downloads.
